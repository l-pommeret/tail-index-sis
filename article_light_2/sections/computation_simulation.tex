\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

Each coordinate is scored in $O(n\log n)$ time: one sort by empirical
rank, then a sliding window over the rank grid whose local Hill statistics
are maintained by a Fenwick tree indexed by global response ranks.
Covariates are generated and processed in blocks, so the total cost is
$O(pn\log n)$ time and $O(n)$ working memory beyond the scores. At
$(n,p)=(2000,1000)$ one complete screen takes under a second on a laptop
core. Scores are sorted increasingly.

\subsection{Simulation models}\label{sec:models}

\paragraph{Family A: tail-index structure.}
Models A1, A2 and A3 share the covariate design, the slowly varying
factor, and the tail-index active set $\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$; they
differ only in the tail-index function. Covariates are latent Gaussian
AR(1),
\[
 Z_{i1}\sim N(0,1),\qquad
 Z_{ij}=\rho Z_{i,j-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\epsilon_{ij},
 \qquad j=2,\ldots,p,
\]
transformed to $U_{ij}=\Phi(Z_{ij})$; the tail-index functions are
evaluated at the population transforms $U$, while the estimator sees
only $Z$ and computes its own empirical ranks. Responses are generated
by quantile inversion, $Y=V^{-\gamma(U)}\ell(1/V,\cdot)$ with
$V\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, the mechanism of
\citet{gardesPodgorny2025}, and all three models carry their slowly
varying factor
\[
 \ell_1(s,u)=\bigl[1+\exp\{v(u)-s\}\bigr]^{-1},
 \qquad
 v(u)=2.5\,(u_5+u_6+u_7+u_8),
\]
which satisfies $\ell_1(s,u)\to1$ as $s\to\infty$: its effect vanishes
in the limit while perturbing estimation at finite tail levels. The
tail-index functions are
\begin{align*}
 \gamma_{A1}(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_{A2}(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-0.5\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_{A3}(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-0.80\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j
 -0.20\,u_1u_2-0.15\,u_3u_4\bigr).
\end{align*}
A1 is the monotone reference, with trimmed population gap $0.186$
(Section~\ref{sec:population}). A2 weakens the signal, with gap
$0.107$. A3 adds interactions among the active coordinates while
keeping a reasonably strong signal, with gap $0.158$. All three share
the maximum $\gmax=0.5$.

\paragraph{Family B: finite-quantile scale contamination.}
Model B1 has the same tail-index function as A1,
$\gamma_{B1}=\gamma_{A1}$, hence the same active set
$\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$ and the same gap $0.186$, and \emph{replaces}
the slowly varying factor: responses are generated with $\ell_2$ below
in place of $\ell_1$, which does not appear in B1. Let
$F_i\sim N(0,1)$ be independent of the active coordinates and of the
base AR(1) innovations, and set
\[
 \log\ell_2(s,F)=-\frac1{2s}+0.5\,\bigl\{\Phi(F)-0.5\bigr\},
\]
so the persistent scale factor lies in $[e^{-0.25},e^{0.25}]$, strictly
positive and uniformly bounded. The factor $F$ is not observed. Instead,
a block of $q_{\rm scale}=20$ proxies occupies the coordinates
$\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,\ldots,24\}$:
\[
 Z_{ij}=0.7\,F_i+\sqrt{1-0.7^2}\,\epsilon_{ij},
 \qquad j\in\A_{\rm scale},
\]
with independent standard normal proxy noises, so each proxy is
marginally standard normal, loads on $F$ at $0.7$, and the pairwise
correlation within the block is $0.49$. The B1 covariate vector is
built from a base AR(1) vector of length $p$: coordinates $1$--$4$ and
$25$--$p$ are kept from the base vector, coordinates $5$--$24$ are
replaced by the proxies, and the AR(1) chain beyond coordinate $24$
continues from the base coordinate $24$, not from the overwritten
proxy. The observed B1 covariance is therefore deliberately not AR(1)
over all coordinates; the AR(1) background is intact outside the proxy
block. Because B1 changes both the slowly varying factor and the
covariate design relative to A1, A1--B1 differences measure the joint
effect of the scale cluster and of the exchange of $\ell_1$ for
$\ell_2$, not the cluster alone.

In B1, variables $1$--$4$ determine the limiting tail index; variables
$5$--$24$ are informative about the finite conditional scale through
their common information about $F$, and do not change the limiting
exponent. A screen built on a fixed high quantile can therefore
legitimately rank the proxy cluster highly --- those variables move its
target --- even though the cluster is inactive for tail-index
screening. B1 measures the practical consequence of this target
difference. 

\paragraph{Monte Carlo design.}
The common design is
\[
 n=2000,\qquad p\in\{500,\,1000,\,2000\},\qquad \rho=0.25,
 \qquad d=20,\qquad \varepsilon=0.05,
\]
with $1{,}000$ replications per cell and per model in the method
comparison (binomial standard errors at most $0.016$) and $200$
common-random-number replications per model in the tuning study
(standard errors at most $0.036$); Sure-$d$ denotes
$\Pp(R_{\max}\le d)$ with $R_{\max}=\max_{j\in\A_\gamma}\mathrm{rank}(j)$,
and all methods within a replication are evaluated on the same dataset.


\subsection{Tuning and the aggregated screen}\label{sec:tuning}

The two smoothing parameters are parameterized as
\[
 \alpha=n^{-a},\qquad h=n^{-b}/2,
\]
following the convention of \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. Sure-20 is
evaluated for each model over the crossed grid
$a\in\{.25,.30,.35,.40,.45,.50\}$,
$b\in\{0,.05,.10,.15,.20,.30,.40\}$ at $n=2000$, $p=1000$,
$\rho=0.25$, on $200$ common-random-number replications per model: the
$200$ datasets are generated once per model and every cell of the grid
is evaluated on the same datasets, so that differences between cells
are not inflated by simulation noise. Figure~\ref{fig:tuning} shows the
four heatmaps.

The column $a=0.25$ collapses for the three A-family models (Sure-20 at
most $.125$, $.080$ and $.225$): at $\alpha=n^{-0.25}\approx0.15$ the
local Hill statistics reach into quantile regions where $\ell_1$, whose
argument $v(u)-s$ is dominated by the $U_5$--$U_8$ combination at
moderate $s$, deforms the projected distributions. B1, whose bounded
scale factor produces no such deformation, degrades only mildly there
(up to $.580$). Performance also falls toward large $a$ and large $b$
together, where the local extreme count collapses, and the degenerate
bandwidth $b=0$ is never competitive. In between, the block
$a\in\{0.30,0.35\}$, $b\in\{0.10,0.15,0.20\}$ is the best-behaved
region of the grid, but it is not a flat plateau. Ordered by the
four-model mean of Sure-20, its six cells run
$0.780$ at $(0.30,0.15)$, $0.773$ at $(0.30,0.10)$, $0.763$ at
$(0.35,0.15)$, $0.756$ at $(0.35,0.10)$, $0.746$ at $(0.30,0.20)$ and
$0.718$ at $(0.35,0.20)$. We adopt
\[
 (a^\star,b^\star)=(0.35,\ 0.15)
\]
rather than the argmax. The three leading cells span $0.017$ on the
four-model mean, of the order of one paired standard error, so they are
not separated by the evidence available here; and they do not trade
uniformly, but along a line that matters for what this paper is about.
On these $200$ replications, moving from
$a=0.30$ to $a=0.35$ costs Sure-20 on the two hardest tail-index models,
$.555\to.475$ on A2 and $.955\to.885$ on A3, and gains on A1,
$.935\to.960$, and on B1, $.675\to.730$. The $1{,}000$-replication
comparison of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is the stronger evidence and
sharpens the picture: on independent data and at all three dimensions the
change costs Sure-20 on \emph{all three} A models and gains on B1, by
$.013$, $.138$ and $.027$ against $.069$ at $p=2000$. The gain on A1 in
the tuning study is within its standard error and does not survive.

That direction is not an accident. A larger $a$ is a smaller tail
fraction, so the local Hill statistics reach less far down into the
distribution --- away from the region where the finite-quantile scale
cluster of B1 moves the conditional distribution without moving the
limiting exponent, and away from the region where $\ell_1$ deforms the
projected distributions in the A family. The choice therefore trades raw
power on the models that carry tail-index structure alone for
selectivity on the model built to separate tail-index activity from
finite-quantile scale effects, which is the distinction the method
exists to make. The cost is concentrated on A2, the weakest signal, and
is stated rather than hidden: per-model Sure-20 at $(0.35,0.15)$ is
$.960$, $.475$, $.885$ and $.730$.

The selection nonetheless optimizes over our own models, and
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} gives the competing screens a grid search
of their own so that this advantage is not left implicit. At $n=2000$
the selected pair gives $\alpha\approx0.070$, $h\approx0.16$.

\paragraph{Relation to the asymptotic theory.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:sis} is an asymptotic exact-recovery result. When $\log(pn)$ is negligible relative to the effective local extreme sample size $n\alpha h$ and to the signal scale $n\alpha h,\Delta_{\min}^2$, all active variables are eventually ranked ahead of the inactive ones, so that Sure-4 tends to one in the present four-signal designs. At the selected tuning and $n=2000$, however, $n\alpha h\approx22$ and $\log(pn)\approx14.5$ at $p=1000$, while for the gap $\Delta_{\min}=0.186$ one has $n\alpha h,\Delta_{\min}^2\approx0.77$. Thus the simulations deliberately consider a finite-sample regime in which these quantities are still comparable, rather than one in which the asymptotic separation is already overwhelming. Their purpose is therefore to examine how informative the ranking remains before exact recovery is reached, and in particular whether retaining a moderately larger set, $d>4$, can still recover all active variables with high probability.

\paragraph{The aggregated screen.}
A single pair $(a,b)$ makes the final ranking depend entirely on one
tuning. The aggregated version of the screen uses the $3\times3$ block
of settings
\[
 \mathcal N_9=\{0.30,0.35,0.40\}\times\{0.10,0.15,0.20\},
\]
the neighbourhood of $(a^\star,b^\star)$ inside the well-behaved region
of the grid. For each setting $(a,b)\in\mathcal N_9$ and each
coordinate $j$, compute the score $\widehat\Psi_j(a,b)$ and its rank
$r_j(a,b)$ among the $p$ coordinates, ranks increasing with the score;
aggregate by
\[
 r_j^{\rm agg}=\min_{(a,b)\in\mathcal N_9}r_j(a,b),
\]
and rank the coordinates by increasing $r_j^{\rm agg}$; coordinates
sharing the same minimum are ordered by the median, then the mean, then
the maximum of their nine ranks, and any remaining ties by a seeded
auxiliary randomization. A coordinate is thus retained early whenever at least
one reasonable tuning finds evidence for it. This direction of
conservatism matches the purpose of a sure-screening first stage: false
exclusion is final, while false retention is corrected by the
second-stage multivariate analysis that the screen feeds. We make no
optimality claim for the construction; it is the procedure used in the
experiments and the application, and its effect is measured against the
single-setting screen in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}. Its cost is nine
score passes instead of one, leaving the $O(pn\log n)$ complexity
unchanged up to the constant. The preceding aggregation does not create an additional asymptotic difficulty. Indeed, since the block \(\mathcal N_9\) contains a fixed number of tuning parameters, the sure-screening result of Theorem~\ref{thm:sis} transfers directly to the minimum-rank aggregation.

\begin{corollary}
Let $\mathcal T$ be a fixed finite set of tuning parameters and define
\[
r_j^{\mathrm{agg}}
=
\min_{(\alpha,h)\in\mathcal T} r_j(\alpha,h).
\]
If the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:sis} hold for every $(\alpha,h)\in\mathcal T$, then, for every $d\ge s$,
\[
\Pr\!\left(A\subseteq \widehat A_d^{\mathrm{agg}}\right)\to 1,
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~3.2, for each $(\alpha,h)\in\mathcal T$, all active coordinates precede all inactive ones with probability tending to one. Since $\mathcal T$ is fixed and finite, this holds simultaneously over $\mathcal T$ with probability tending to one. Taking the minimum rank over $\mathcal T$ preserves this separation.
\end{proof}

The two versions are reported side by side throughout:
\emph{Tail-index SIS, selected $(a^\star,b^\star)$} uses the central
pair alone; \emph{Tail-index SIS, aggregated} uses $r_j^{\rm agg}$.

\subsection{Comparison with competing screens}\label{sec:comparison}

Seven rules are compared on the same $1{,}000$ simulated datasets per
model and per dimension $p\in\{500,1000,2000\}$, at $n=2000$,
$\rho=0.25$: the two versions of the tail-index screen; two versions of
the conditional-Pickands screen of \citet{yoshidaUmezu2026}; and the
quantile-adaptive SIS of \citet{heWangHong2013} at the levels
$\tau\in\{.90,.95,.99\}$, using cubic B-spline marginal quantile
regression with three degrees of freedom, identical at every level. The
competitors rank large utilities first; ours ranks small scores first.

The conditional-Pickands screen is implemented from equations (2)--(4)
of \citet{yoshidaUmezu2026}, with their Epanechnikov kernel and 25
equally spaced evaluation points in $[.02,.98]$. It is reported twice.
\emph{Paper tuning} transplants the setting of their numerical work
proportionally: their bandwidth $h=1$ on standardized covariates is
kept, and their extreme-value count is carried over as the same
fraction of the sample size, $k=\lfloor0.072\,n\rfloor$. \emph{Tuned per
model} instead gives that screen a grid search of its own, matching the
one performed for the tail-index screen in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}:
Sure-20 is evaluated over
\[
 h\in\{0.5,\ 1,\ 1.5,\ 2,\ 3\},
 \qquad
 k/n\in\{.050,\ .060,\ .072,\ .080\},
\]
on the same $200$ common-random-number replications per model that were
used to tune our own screen, and the best cell is retained. The grid is
chosen to be coherent with the sensitivity study of
\citet{yoshidaUmezu2026}: their bandwidth range is $[0.04,10]$, with
$h<0.1$ unusable, $h=1.5$--$2.0$ best and $h>2$ progressively
oversmoothed, and their intermediate sequence spans $k\in[121,200]$ at
$n=2500$, i.e.\ fractions $[.048,.080]$, around a baseline of $.072$. The
grid is deliberately not widened beyond what they examine: a search over
values their own study rules out would no longer be their method being
tuned.

One consequence is worth recording. On these designs the retained cell
sits at the \emph{low} end of their range for three of the four models
--- $h=0.5$ on A1 and A3, $k/n=.050$ on B1 --- rather than in the
$h=1.5$--$2.0$ region their own sensitivity study identifies as best.
Their recommendation was reached on their model and with a different
criterion, the selection rate of individual covariates rather than the
joint event that all four active covariates are retained; A1 nonetheless
carries their tail-index function, so the discrepancy is not explained by
a change of signal. What the grid shows is that the tuning advice of
\citet{yoshidaUmezu2026} does not transfer to the joint criterion used
here, and that their screen improves substantially when it is retuned
for it.

Two asymmetries deliberately favour the competitor in this comparison.
Its grid cell is selected \emph{separately for each model}, whereas the
tail-index screen is reported at a single $(a^\star,b^\star)$ common to
all four; and its selection is made on Sure-20 itself, the criterion on
which it is then judged. Reporting both versions makes the size of the
resulting advantage visible rather than leaving it implicit.
Tables~\ref{tab:cmp500}--\ref{tab:cmp2000} report, for each model and
method, Sure-4 (exact recovery), Sure-20, and the mean and median of the
worst active rank.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the dimension.}
Every method degrades as $p$ grows, at very different rates, and the
mean--median contrast locates the degradation. For the selected-pair
tail screen on A1, $\E(R_{\max})$ grows from $6.6$ to $15.8$ between
$p=500$ and $p=2000$ while $\mathrm{Med}(R_{\max})$ stays at $4$: the
typical replication is unaffected, and the loss is carried by a
minority of replications in which an active coordinate falls into the
null range, where its rank scales with $p$. Aggregation compresses that
tail on every model ($\E(R_{\max})$ $4.6\to6.2$ on A1, $9.8\to22.6$
against $27.5\to75.9$ on B1, $5.1\to8.1$ against $9.8\to30.1$ on A3).
The quantile screen at $\tau=.95$ is the least dimension-sensitive rule
on the A family ($\E(R_{\max})$ $4.2\to4.8$ on A1). A2 is the most
dimension-sensitive model for every method (aggregated Sure-20
$.797\to.561$, quantile $.95$ $.930\to.782$): a weak signal loses the
most from added competition. The conditional-Pickands screen degrades
from a low base throughout: at its paper tuning
$\mathrm{Med}(R_{\max})$ moves from $29$ to $168$ on A1, and its own
grid search reduces the level of the degradation without changing its
slope ($8$ to $12$ on the same model).

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the quantile level.}
On the A family the quantile screen is non-monotone in $\tau$ in every
table, in opposite directions on either side of $\tau=.95$; on B1 its
behaviour tracks its target, and Table~\ref{tab:b1comp} reports the
resulting composition of its leading positions. At $\tau=.90$ its top
four contain on average $3.8$ of the twenty scale proxies and all
twenty fill its top 24; exact recovery is zero to three decimals at
every $p$. This is coherent behaviour for a $.90$-quantile target,
which the proxies genuinely move, and it is fatal for tail-index
recovery. At $\tau=.95$ the contamination remains substantial
($2.4$--$2.5$ proxies in the top four, $19.1$--$19.5$ in the top 24,
median worst active rank $20$--$21$). At $\tau=.99$ the contamination
largely clears ($0.2$--$0.4$ proxies in the top four) because the
quantile target approaches the tail index, but the variance cost is
severe everywhere: on the A family, Sure-4 falls from $.234$--$.891$
at $\tau=.95$ to at most $.125$ at $\tau=.99$, and on B1 the level
$.99$ never reaches Sure-4 $.04$. For tail-index screening, this sensitivity to $\tau$ is an important
distinction. Quantile-SIS requires a quantile level to be fixed in
advance, and there is no reason for the level that best approximates the
tail-index ranking to be known a priori. In the A family, $\tau=.95$
happens to give very good performance and can outperform the tail-index
screen in some settings, but moving to either $\tau=.90$ or $\tau=.99$
can substantially reduce recovery. The tail-index screen does not require
this additional choice. This avoids a
source of sensitivity that is specific to using fixed high quantiles as
proxies for tail-index activity.

\paragraph{Effect of the aggregation.}
Aggregation improves the tail screen most where failures are worst. At the
selected pair it improves \emph{both} criteria in all twelve
model--dimension cells. Sure-4 gains most on the models where the
single-setting screen is already strong ($+8.4$, $+7.7$, $+10.2$ points
on A1 and $+6.3$, $+5.0$, $+9.6$ on A3, across the three dimensions),
and least on the weak-signal A2 ($+2.6$, $+3.6$, $+0.8$); on B1 it
lifts exact recovery from $.377/.336/.312$ to $.448/.368/.339$.
$\E(R_{\max})$ is divided by a factor between $1.4$ and $3.7$
everywhere, and $\mathrm{Med}(R_{\max})$ falls to $4$ on A1 and A3 and
to $5$--$6$ on B1.

The largest gains are in inclusion rather than exact selection, and
they are concentrated where the single-setting screen fails. On A2 at
$p=2000$, Sure-20 rises from $.325$ to $.561$ and $\E(R_{\max})$ falls
from $248.8$ to $76.9$; on B1 at the same dimension, from $.668$ to
$.805$ and from $75.9$ to $22.6$. This is the intended behaviour:
promotion by a single reasonable tuning rescues active coordinates from
setting-specific failures far down the list, which is what moves the
sure-screening criterion, while the top four is already close to
saturated on the models where the signal is strong.

The quantile screen at a moderate level remains the strongest detector
when high quantiles and the limiting exponent share their covariates
and the signal is weak (A2). On A1 and A3 the two are close, with
$\tau=.95$ slightly ahead on A3 and the two changing places on A1 as
$p$ grows. The separation appears on B1, where the aggregated tail
screen is the only rule whose leading positions stay on the active set
--- Sure-4 $.448/.368/.339$ and Sure-20 $.907/.849/.805$ across the
three dimensions, against at most $.038$ and $.665$ for any
fixed-quantile level, with $0.12$--$0.18$ proxies in its top four
(Table~\ref{tab:b1comp}). The competitor's grid search is worth a
remark here, because it cuts the other way: retuning the
conditional-Pickands screen for Sure-20 raises the number of scale
proxies in its top four on B1 from $1.78$--$1.35$ to $2.35$--$2.09$
across the three dimensions. What the retuning buys in breadth of
screening it gives back in specificity against the finite-quantile
cluster, which is the discrimination at issue in this model. It is
degraded, not
immune: the same rule reaches Sure-4 $.884/.823/.802$ on A1, so the
scale cluster, jointly with the exchange of slowly varying factors,
costs it about forty-five points of exact recovery. For a question
about the limiting exponent itself, the two rankings are best read
side by side, as in Section~\ref{sec:realdata}: variables high on one
list and low on the other are where the two targets part ways.
