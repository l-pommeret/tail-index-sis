\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

Each coordinate is scored in $O(n\log n)$ time: one sort by empirical
rank, then a sliding window over the rank grid whose local Hill statistics
are maintained by a Fenwick tree indexed by global response ranks.
Covariates are generated and processed in blocks, so the total cost is
$O(pn\log n)$ time and $O(n)$ working memory beyond the scores. At
$(n,p)=(2000,1000)$ one complete screen takes under a second on a laptop
core. Scores are sorted increasingly, ties broken by coordinate index; a
coordinate whose grid is entirely underpopulated receives score $+\infty$.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

All four models share the covariate design and the active set
$\A=\{1,2,3,4\}$. Latent Gaussian AR(1) covariates
$Z_{ij}=\rho Z_{i,j-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\epsilon_{ij}$ are transformed to
$U_{ij}=\Phi(Z_{ij})$; the tail-index functions are evaluated at the
population transforms $U$, while the estimator sees only $Z$ and computes
its own empirical ranks. Responses are generated by quantile inversion,
\[
 Y=V^{-\gamma(U)}\,\ell(1/V,U),\qquad V\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1),
\]
the mechanism used by \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. We combine three
tail-index functions from increasing difficulty,
\begin{align*}
 \gamma_1(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_2(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-0.5\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_3(u)&=0.55\exp\bigl(-0.35\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j
 -0.20\,u_1u_2-0.15\,u_3u_4\bigr),
\end{align*}
with two slowly varying functions in the forms of
\citet[Section~4.3]{gardesPodgorny2025},
\[
 \ell^{(1)}(s,u)=\bigl[1+\exp\{v(u)-s\}\bigr]^{-1},
 \qquad
 \ell^{(2)}(s,u)=e^{-1/(2s)}\,v_\kappa(u),
\]
where $v(u)=2.5\,(u_5+u_6+u_7+u_8)\in(0,10)$ matches the range of their
$B_1^\top x$, and the scale factor of $\ell^{(2)}$ is
\[
 v_\kappa(u)=\exp\Bigl\{\kappa\sum_{j=5}^{8}\bigl(u_j-\tfrac12\bigr)\Bigr\}
 \in[e^{-2\kappa},e^{2\kappa}],
\]
strictly positive, bounded, symmetric in the four \emph{inactive}
variables, and controlled by the single strength parameter $\kappa$;
its bounds keep the model compatible with the uniform constants in
(C2). The four models are
\[
 \mathrm{M1}=(\gamma_1,\ell^{(1)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M2}=(\gamma_1,\ell^{(2)}_{\kappa^\star}),\quad
 \mathrm{M3}=(\gamma_2,\ell^{(1)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M4}=(\gamma_3,\ell^{(1)}).
\]
M1 is the reference. M2 keeps the tail index of M1 and changes only the
slowly varying factor: $\ell^{(2)}(s,u)\to v_\kappa(u)$ as $s\to\infty$,
so the tail index depends on $\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$ while the scale ---
hence every high finite quantile --- also depends on
$\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,6,7,8\}$. Variables can therefore matter for a
$0.95$-quantile screen without entering the limiting exponent, by
construction; Section~\ref{sec:comparison} exploits this. The strength
$\kappa^\star=0.20$ was chosen by a pilot on a dedicated seed stream,
never reused below: candidate values
$\kappa\in\{.15,.25,.35,.50,.70\}$, plus the single authorized midpoint
extension $\kappa=.20$, were evaluated on 80 replications each. The
selection rule, fixed in the pilot code before inspection, required the
smallest $\kappa$ with (i) tail-screen Sure-20 at least $0.5$, (ii)
clearly more scale variables in the quantile screen's top four than in
the tail screen's, (iii) more of $\A_\gamma$ in the tail screen's top
four than in the quantile screen's, and (iv) at least one scale
variable in the quantile screen's top four on average. No candidate
satisfied all four: at $\kappa=.20$, criteria (i), (ii) and (iv) hold
(Sure-20 $0.55$, with standard error $0.056$ on 80 replications, so
(i) itself clears its threshold only within one standard error;
$1.14$ against $0.17$ scale variables) but (iii) fails by a margin
within Monte Carlo error ($2.71$ against $2.83$ of $\A_\gamma$), while
at $\kappa=.25$ criterion (i) fails ($0.40$). We therefore applied the
protocol's declared fallback --- the candidate best balancing scale
separation against tail estimability --- and fixed
$\kappa^\star=0.20$; the choice is a judgment call informed by the
pilot, not the output of a rule that selected uniquely. Full pilot
results are in the supplementary table (\texttt{pilot\_summary.csv}). M3 halves the exponent range of the
signal, weakening the population gaps from $0.186$ to $0.107$; M4 adds
interactions among the active coordinates while remaining detectable
(Proposition~\ref{prop:geometry}), with trimmed gap $0.086$.

None of the four models belongs to the sufficient classes of
Appendix~\ref{app:examples}: their tail-index surfaces are continuous
with maximizers at a corner of the active cube, so the fibre-maximizer
laws have no atom and the projected quantiles acquire slow variation of
logarithmic type --- the boundary regime in which condition (E2) covers
only gaps large relative to $1/\log n$. At $n=2000$ the corresponding
bias modulus is of order one, larger than every population gap
($0.086$--$0.186$), so the finite-sample bounds of
Section~\ref{sec:estimation} are not numerically informative for these
designs. The simulations therefore probe the method beyond the regime
its theory certifies. Ranking nevertheless succeeds because much of the
local Hill bias is shared across coordinates --- every inactive
coordinate estimates the same projected mixture --- and the screen uses
only score differences; the experiments should be read as evidence
about this common-mode behaviour, which the theory does not exploit,
rather than as confirmation of the bounds.

Unless a factor is explicitly varied, the design is
\[
 n=2000,\qquad p=1000,\qquad \rho=0.25,\qquad d=20,
 \qquad \varepsilon=0.05,
\]
with 200 Monte Carlo replications per cell; Sure-$d$ denotes
$\Pp(R_{\max}\le d)$ with $R_{\max}=\max_{j\in\A}\mathrm{rank}(j)$, and
binomial standard errors are at most $0.036$.

\subsection{Tuning}\label{sec:tuning}

The two smoothing parameters are parameterized as
\[
 \alpha=n^{-a},\qquad h=n^{-b}/2,
\]
following the convention of \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. We evaluate
Sure-20 for each of the four models over the crossed grid
$a\in\{.25,.30,.35,.40,.45,.50\}$,
$b\in\{0,.05,.10,.15,.20,.30,.40\}$ at the baseline design;
Figure~\ref{fig:tuning} shows the four heatmaps.

Three features stand out. The row $a=0.25$ collapses for the three
$\ell^{(1)}$ models (Sure-20 at most $.115$, $.085$ and $.060$ for M1,
M3 and M4) but not for M2 (up to $.475$): at
$\alpha=n^{-0.25}\approx0.15$ the local Hill statistics reach into
quantile regions where the factor $\ell^{(1)}$, whose argument
$v(u)-s$ is dominated by the $U_5$--$U_8$ combination $v(u)\in(0,10)$
at moderate $s$, deforms the projected distributions; the recalibrated
$\ell^{(2)}$, bounded within $[e^{-0.4},e^{0.4}]$, produces no such
deformation. Performance also degrades toward large $a$ and large $b$
together, where the local extreme count collapses, and the degenerate
bandwidth $b=0$, which retains half of the rank scale in every window,
is never optimal. In between, the row $a=0.30$ performs best for M1,
M3 and M4 simultaneously over $b\in\{.05,\ldots,.20\}$, with Sure-20
ranges $(.915$--$.975,\ .445$--$.605,\ .300$--$.385)$.
The M1 variation over this region is small ($0.06$, within two to
three standard errors of a difference); the M3 and M4 variations are
larger (up to $0.16$, about three standard errors of a difference),
so the region is one of good rather than statistically
indistinguishable performance, and we select the value of $b$ at which
both M3 and M4 attain their grid maxima. We fix
\[
 (a^\star,b^\star)=(0.30,\ 0.10),
 \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
 \alpha=n^{-0.3},\quad h=n^{-0.1}/2,
\]
for every subsequent experiment, in simulation and in the application; at
$n=2000$ this gives $\alpha\approx0.10$, $h\approx0.23$, and about
$2n\alpha h\approx96$ effective local extremes per interior window,
whose width is $2h$. No model is given its own tuning.

M2 behaves like a damped M1: its Sure-20 reaches $0.555$ at
$(a^\star,b^\star)$ and its grid maximum, $0.625$, sits at the deeper
tail fraction $a=0.40$ with the same $b=0.10$ --- the direction
predicted by condition (E2), since a smaller $\alpha$ reduces the bias
contributed by $\ell^{(2)}$. M2 uses the common tuning, at a cost of
$0.07$ relative to its own best cell; its role in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is to separate statistical targets.

Two caveats on this selection protocol apply throughout. Sure-20 is an
oracle criterion --- it requires knowing the active set --- so the
choice of $(a^\star,b^\star)$ is a simulation-calibrated default, not a
data-driven rule; no such rule is proposed in this paper, and the
half-sample stability diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:realdata} are the
practical substitute. Second, for the $\ell^{(1)}$ models $a^\star=0.30$ sits directly
above the collapsed row $a=0.25$, so moderate misspecification of $a$
toward larger tail fractions is the dangerous direction there ---
model-dependently, since M2 is robust at $a=0.25$; the real-data
stability grid includes $a=0.25$ deliberately.

\subsection{Influence of the design factors}\label{sec:factors}

This subsection uses Model M1 only, varies one factor at a time around the
baseline $(n,p,\rho,d)=(2000,1000,0.25,20)$, on a separate seed stream
from the tuning study. At the baseline, Sure-20 is $0.950$ and
the mean worst active rank is $9.2$; the same design cell appears in the
tuning grid ($0.935$) and in the comparison below ($0.925$), each from
its own replications, and the three values agree within Monte Carlo
error. Figure~\ref{fig:factors} displays
the three one-factor curves.

Sample size acts through the local extreme count, in the direction the rate suggests:
Sure-20 rises from $0.400$ at $n=1000$ to $0.950$ at $n=2000$ and
$1.000$ at $n=5000$. The ambient dimension degrades performance slowly:
$0.995$, $0.960$, $0.950$, $0.905$ at $p=100$, $500$, $1000$, $5000$
--- a five-fold increase of $p$ beyond the baseline costs less than five
points of screening probability, consistent with the logarithmic entry
of $p$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:sis}. Dependence helps in this design:
Sure-20 is $0.615$ at $\rho=0$ and reaches $1.000$ at $\rho\ge0.5$. The
active block $\{1,2,3,4\}$ is contiguous in the AR(1) chain, so positive
correlation concentrates observations near the diagonal of the active
cube, where the envelope is estimated; the direction of this effect is
design-specific and correlated inactive neighbours of the active block
can equally be promoted in finite samples.

Figure~\ref{fig:sured} shows $d\mapsto\Pp(R_{\max}\le d)$ at the
baseline; the ranking underlying the curve does not depend on $d$. The
curve starts at $0.805$ for $d=4$, which is exact recovery since
$|\A|=4$, passes $0.950$ at the default $d=20$, and reaches $0.990$ by
$d=100$. A moderate retained dimension therefore buys near-certain
inclusion, which is the intended reading of a screening guarantee.

\subsection{Comparison with competing screens}\label{sec:comparison}

Three methods are compared on the same 200 simulated datasets per model at
the baseline design: the proposed tail-index screen with the tuning
$(a^\star,b^\star)$ fixed in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}; the
conditional-Pickands screen of \citet{yoshidaUmezu2026}, implemented from
their equations (2)--(4) with the Epanechnikov kernel and the tuning of
their numerical work transplanted proportionally
($h=1$, $k=\lfloor0.072\,n\rfloor$), on 25 equally spaced evaluation
points in $[.02,.98]$; the bandwidth sensitivity below varies $h$ only,
with $k$ kept at their ratio; and the quantile-adaptive SIS of
\citet{heWangHong2013}, using cubic B-spline marginal quantile regression
with three degrees of freedom, identical at every quantile level, its
utility being the empirical squared deviation of the fitted conditional
quantile from the marginal one. The two competitors rank large utilities
first; ours ranks small scores first. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} reports
the levels $\tau=.95$ and $\tau=.99$; Section~\ref{sec:tau} varies
$\tau$ over $\{.90,.95,.975,.99\}$ on the same datasets.

At $\tau=.95$, quantile SIS performs best in M1, M3 and M4 (Sure-20
$1.000$, $.815$ and $.690$ against our $.925$, $.535$ and $.345$),
where the monotone $\gamma$ moves the entire upper distribution and the
quantile utility pools all $n=2000$ observations at a moderately extreme
level, while the tail-index statistic works from about
$2n\alpha h\approx96$ local extremes per window. At $\tau=.99$ the
ordering reverses on all four models (our $.925$, $.570$, $.535$,
$.345$ against $.610$, $.395$, $.085$, $.010$). The Pickands-based
screen of Yoshida--Umezu trails on all four models at the transplanted
tuning; rerunning it at bandwidths $h\in\{0.5,1.5\}$ on the same
datasets does not change its position (best Sure-20 over the three
bandwidths: $.445$, $.270$, $.165$, $.160$, the M2 value at $h=1.5$).

M2 separates the two statistical targets. Its tail index depends on
$\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$ only, while the scale factor
$v_{\kappa^\star}$ carries $\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,6,7,8\}$ into every
finite quantile. Table~\ref{tab:composition} shows the composition of
each method's four leading positions. The quantile screen at
$\tau=.90$ places on average $2.28$ scale variables in its top four ---
correct behaviour for its own target, since those variables genuinely
move the $.90$ quantile --- and at $\tau=.95$ still $1.12$. The
tail-index screen places $0.16$. Both recover $\A_\gamma$ in their top
four at nearly the same rate ($2.87$ for the tail screen, $2.82$ for
$\tau=.95$), so on M2 the practical difference between the two screens
is not power but what the leading positions contain: an analyst reading
a $\tau=.95$ ranking as a statement about the limiting tail would carry
forward one or two variables that do not determine it. As $\tau$
increases the quantile target approaches the tail index and the scale
contamination falls ($.35$ at $\tau=.975$, $.03$ at $\tau=.99$), but
the variance cost rises faster: Sure-20 drops from $.940$ at
$\tau=.95$ to $.395$ at $\tau=.99$, below our $.570$.

\subsection{Sensitivity of quantile SIS to the level}\label{sec:tau}

Figure~\ref{fig:tau} plots Sure-20 of the quantile screen against
$\tau\in\{.90,.95,.975,.99\}$ for the four models, on the same datasets
as Table~\ref{tab:comparison}. Two forms of sensitivity combine. First,
changing $\tau$ changes the statistical target $Q_\tau(Y\mid X)$: under
M2, lower levels weight the scale variables more heavily, which is
visible in Table~\ref{tab:composition}. Second, raising $\tau$ thins
the information available for estimating the target: every curve is
highest at $\tau=.90$ or $.95$ and collapses by $\tau=.99$
(M3: $.815$ at $.95$, $.085$ at $.99$; M4: $.690$ to $.010$). The
tail-index screen is subject to a bias--variance compromise of the same
kind through $\alpha$, but with a fixed asymptotic target: moving
$\alpha$ changes the threshold used to estimate $\gamma$, not the
definition of $\gamma$. Neither method is tuning-free; the tunings mean
different things.

The comparison supports a conditional recommendation. For a scientific
question about a fixed high quantile, or when high quantiles and the
limiting exponent can be expected to share their covariates, the
quantile screen at a moderate level exploits the full sample and is the
better tool. For a question about the limiting exponent itself ---
which covariates change the polynomial rate of decay --- the tail-index
screen targets that quantity directly, keeps scale variables out of its
leading positions, and avoids the variance collapse that affects the
quantile screen at genuinely extreme levels. Reading the two rankings
side by side, as in Section~\ref{sec:realdata}, identifies the
variables on which the two questions part ways.
