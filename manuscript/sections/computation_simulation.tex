\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

Each coordinate is scored in $O(n\log n)$ time: one sort by empirical
rank, then a sliding window over the rank grid whose local Hill statistics
are maintained by a Fenwick tree indexed by global response ranks.
Covariates are generated and processed in blocks, so the total cost is
$O(pn\log n)$ time and $O(n)$ working memory beyond the scores. At
$(n,p)=(2000,1000)$ one complete screen takes under a second on a laptop
core. Scores are sorted increasingly, ties broken by coordinate index; a
coordinate whose grid is entirely underpopulated receives score $+\infty$.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

All four models share the covariate design and the tail-index active set
$\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$. Latent Gaussian AR(1) covariates
$Z_{ij}=\rho Z_{i,j-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\epsilon_{ij}$ are transformed to
$U_{ij}=\Phi(Z_{ij})$; the tail-index functions are evaluated at the
population transforms $U$, while the estimator sees only $Z$ and computes
its own empirical ranks. Responses are generated by quantile inversion,
\[
 Y=V^{-\gamma(U)}\,\ell(1/V,\cdot),\qquad V\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1),
\]
the mechanism used by \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. The tail-index
functions are
\begin{align*}
 \gamma_1(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_2(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-0.5\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_3(u)&=0.55\exp\bigl(-0.80\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j
 -0.20\,u_1u_2-0.15\,u_3u_4\bigr).
\end{align*}

Models M1, M3 and M4 carry the slowly varying factor of
\citet[Section~4.3]{gardesPodgorny2025},
\[
 \ell^{(1)}(s,u)=\bigl[1+\exp\{v(u)-s\}\bigr]^{-1},
 \qquad
 v(u)=2.5\,(u_5+u_6+u_7+u_8)\in(0,10),
\]
matching the range of their $B_1^\top x$; since $\ell^{(1)}\to1$ as
$s\to\infty$, its effect vanishes in the tail.

Model M2 instead carries a scale factor that persists at every finite
quantile. A single latent standard normal factor $F$, independent of the
active coordinates and not itself observed, enters the response through
\[
 \log\ell^{(2)}(s,F)=-\frac1{2s}
 +\kappa_c\bigl\{\Phi(F)-\tfrac12\bigr\},
 \qquad \kappa_c=0.4772,
\]
so that the scale part of $\ell^{(2)}$ is strictly positive, bounded
within $[e^{-\kappa_c/2},e^{\kappa_c/2}]$ --- compatible with the
uniform constants in (C2) --- and has
$\operatorname{Var}\bigl[\kappa_c\{\Phi(F)-\tfrac12\}\bigr]
=\kappa_c^2/12$ exactly. The analyst observes $s=20$ proxies of $F$,
occupying the coordinates $\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,\ldots,24\}$,
\[
 Z_{ij}=\lambda F_i+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\,\epsilon_{ij},
 \qquad j\in\A_{\rm scale},\qquad \lambda=0.7,
\]
each marginally standard normal like every other covariate and correlated
with the nuisance at level $\lambda$ (pairwise intra-cluster correlation
$\lambda^2=0.49$). The four models are
\[
 \mathrm{M1}=(\gamma_1,\ell^{(1)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M2}=(\gamma_1,\ell^{(2)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M3}=(\gamma_2,\ell^{(1)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M4}=(\gamma_3,\ell^{(1)}).
\]

Each model isolates one difficulty. M1 is the monotone reference. M2
keeps the tail index of M1 and adds the persistent scale cluster: its
tail index depends on $\A_\gamma$ only, while every finite conditional
quantile also depends on the twenty scale coordinates, which are
individually attractive to any screen built on finite quantiles yet
carry no information about the limiting exponent. M3 halves the exponent
range of the signal, weakening the trimmed population gaps from $0.186$
(M1, M2) to $0.107$. M4 combines a strong monotone signal (trimmed gap
$0.173$) with interactions among the active coordinates, so it probes
departures from additivity rather than weakness of signal. All four
models are implemented in \texttt{generate4.R}, with
$(\kappa_c,s,\lambda)=(0.4772,20,0.7)$ fixed throughout.

None of the four models satisfies the projected-quantile conditions
(E1)--(E3) with gaps of the order required at $n=2000$: their tail-index
surfaces are maximized at a corner of the active cube with no atom in
the fibre-maximizer law, which places the projected quantiles in the
logarithmic slow-variation regime discussed after (E3) in
Section~\ref{sec:estimation}, where the bias modulus at $n=2000$ is of
order one, larger than every population gap. The simulations therefore
probe the method beyond the regime its finite-sample theory certifies.
Ranking nevertheless succeeds because much of the local Hill bias is
shared across coordinates --- every inactive coordinate estimates the
same projected mixture --- and the screen uses only score differences;
the experiments measure this common-mode behaviour, which the theory
does not exploit.

The common Monte Carlo design is
\[
 n=2000,\qquad p\in\{500,\,1000,\,2000\},\qquad \rho=0.25,
 \qquad d=20,\qquad \varepsilon=0.05,
\]
with $1{,}000$ replications per cell and per model in the method
comparison (binomial standard errors at most $0.016$) and $40$
replications per cell in the tuning study (standard errors at most
$0.079$); Sure-$d$ denotes $\Pp(R_{\max}\le d)$ with
$R_{\max}=\max_{j\in\A_\gamma}\mathrm{rank}(j)$, and all methods within
a replication are evaluated on the same dataset.

\subsection{Tuning and the aggregated screen}\label{sec:tuning}

The two smoothing parameters are parameterized as
\[
 \alpha=n^{-a},\qquad h=n^{-b}/2,
\]
following the convention of \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. We evaluate
Sure-20 for each of the four models over the crossed grid
$a\in\{.25,.30,.35,.40,.45,.50\}$,
$b\in\{0,.05,.10,.15,.20,.30,.40\}$ at $n=2000$, $p=1000$,
$\rho=0.25$; Figure~\ref{fig:tuning} shows the four heatmaps.

Three features stand out. The row $a=0.25$ collapses for the three
$\ell^{(1)}$ models (Sure-20 at most $.095$, $.050$ and $.200$ for M1,
M3 and M4 over $b\le.20$) but not for M2 (up to $.475$): at
$\alpha=n^{-0.25}\approx0.15$ the local Hill statistics reach into
quantile regions where $\ell^{(1)}$, whose argument $v(u)-s$ is
dominated by the $U_5$--$U_8$ combination at moderate $s$, deforms the
projected distributions, while the bounded scale factor of M2 produces
no such deformation. Performance degrades toward large $a$ and large
$b$ together, where the local extreme count collapses, and the
degenerate bandwidth $b=0$ is never competitive. In between, the row
$a=0.30$ is best for M1, M3 and M4 simultaneously, and
\[
 (a^\star,b^\star)=(0.30,\ 0.15)
\]
attains the highest four-model mean Sure-20 on the grid ($0.796$,
against $0.785$ for $(0.30,0.10)$), with per-model values
$.965$, $.710$, $.585$ and $.925$. We fix $(a^\star,b^\star)$ for every
subsequent experiment, in simulation and in the application; at
$n=2000$ this gives $\alpha\approx0.10$, $h\approx0.16$, and about
$2n\alpha h\approx64$ effective local extremes per interior window. No
model is given its own tuning.

\paragraph{The aggregated screen.}
A single pair $(a,b)$ makes the final ranking depend entirely on one
tuning. To stabilize the ranking against small variations of the
tuning, we also evaluate an aggregated version of the screen, built on
the $3\times3$ block of settings
\[
 \mathcal N_9=\{0.30,0.35,0.40\}\times\{0.10,0.15,0.20\},
\]
the neighbourhood of $(a^\star,b^\star)$ within the well-behaved region
of the grid (the row $a=0.25$, which collapses for three of the four
models, is excluded). For each setting $(a,b)\in\mathcal N_9$ and each
coordinate $j$, compute the score $\widehat\Psi_j(a,b)$ and its rank
$r_j(a,b)$ among the $p$ coordinates, ranks increasing with the score;
aggregate by
\[
 r_j^{\rm agg}=\min_{(a,b)\in\mathcal N_9}r_j(a,b),
\]
and rank the coordinates by increasing $r_j^{\rm agg}$, ties broken by
coordinate index. A coordinate is thus retained early if at least one
setting of the block ranks it early. We make no optimality claim for
this construction; it is the procedure used in the experiments below and
in the application, and its effect is measured against the
single-setting screen in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}. Its cost is nine
score passes instead of one, leaving the $O(pn\log n)$ complexity
unchanged up to the constant.

The two versions are reported side by side throughout:
\emph{Tail-index SIS, selected $(a^\star,b^\star)$} uses the central
pair alone; \emph{Tail-index SIS, aggregated} uses $r_j^{\rm agg}$.

\subsection{Comparison with competing screens}\label{sec:comparison}

Six rules are compared on the same $1{,}000$ simulated datasets per
model and per dimension $p\in\{500,1000,2000\}$, at $n=2000$,
$\rho=0.25$: the two versions of the tail-index screen; the
conditional-Pickands screen of \citet{yoshidaUmezu2026}, implemented
from their equations (2)--(4) with the Epanechnikov kernel and the
tuning of their numerical work transplanted proportionally ($h=1$,
$k=\lfloor0.072\,n\rfloor$), on 25 equally spaced evaluation points in
$[.02,.98]$; and the quantile-adaptive SIS of \citet{heWangHong2013} at
the levels $\tau\in\{.90,.95,.99\}$, using cubic B-spline marginal
quantile regression with three degrees of freedom, identical at every
level. The competitors rank large utilities first; ours ranks small
scores first. Tables~\ref{tab:cmp500}--\ref{tab:cmp2000} report, for
each model and method, Sure-4 (exact recovery), Sure-20, and the mean
and median of the worst active rank.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the dimension.}
Read across the three tables, the dimension degrades every method, but
at very different rates, and the mean--median contrast identifies the
mechanism. For the single-setting tail screen on M1,
$\E(R_{\max})$ grows from $5.7$ to $12.9$ as $p$ goes from $500$ to
$2000$ while $\mathrm{Med}(R_{\max})$ stays at $4$: the typical
replication is unaffected by the extra competitors, and the degradation
is carried by a minority of replications in which an active coordinate
falls into the null range, where its rank scales with $p$. Aggregation
compresses exactly that tail ($\E(R_{\max})$ $4.6\to6.1$ on M1,
$8.8\to19.5$ against $26.9\to98.8$ on M2), and the quantile screen at
$\tau=.95$ is the least dimension-sensitive on the $\ell^{(1)}$ models
($4.2\to4.8$ on M1). M3 is the most dimension-sensitive model for every
method (Sure-20 of the aggregated screen $.813\to.580$, of quantile
$.95$ $.930\to.782$): a weak signal loses the most from additional
competition. Yoshida--Umezu degrades from a low base on all four
models.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the quantile level.}
Within every table, the quantile screen is non-monotone in $\tau$, and
in opposite directions on either side of $\tau=.95$. At $\tau=.90$ it
fails on M2 in the most instructive way: its top four contain on
average $3.7$ scale variables, and all twenty scale coordinates fill
its top 24, so exact recovery is essentially zero --- correct behaviour
for its own target, since those variables genuinely move the $.90$
quantile, and fatal for tail-index recovery. Raising $\tau$ to $.99$
almost eliminates the scale contamination ($0.18$--$0.41$ scale
variables in the top four) because the quantile target approaches the
tail index; the variance cost, however, is severe on every model
(Sure-4 at most $.125$ across models at $p=500$ and lower at
$p=2000$). The level $\tau=.95$ is the best compromise on M1, M3 and
M4, and on none of the three levels does the quantile screen approach
the tail screen on M2 (Sure-4 at most $.073$ against $.431$ at
$p=2000$; median worst rank $17$--$18$ against $4$--$5$, the signature
of one to two scale variables permanently occupying its leading
positions). The direction of the $\tau$-sensitivity does not depend on
$p$; its amplitude grows with $p$ on the weak-signal M3.
Yoshida--Umezu, which estimates the index through Pickands-type
quantile differences at its own fixed levels, is dominated on all
models and dimensions here, with $6.4$--$9.7$ of the twenty M2 scale
coordinates in its top 24.

\paragraph{Effect of the aggregation.}
The aggregated screen improves on the selected pair almost uniformly,
and most where failures are worst. On M2 it lifts exact recovery from
$.331/.290/.263$ to $.549/.466/.431$ across the three dimensions and
divides $\E(R_{\max})$ by three to five; on M1 and M4 it adds two to
seven points of Sure-4 and compresses the rank tail (M4 at $p=2000$:
$\E(R_{\max})$ $21.6\to8.0$); Sure-20 improves in all twelve
model--dimension cells. The one systematic exception is exact recovery
on the weak-signal M3, where aggregation trades Sure-4 away
($.256\to.245$ at $p=500$, $.149\to.113$ at $p=2000$) while still
improving Sure-20 ($.463\to.580$ at $p=2000$) and
$\E(R_{\max})$ ($144.8\to76.0$): the minimum promotes any coordinate
that some setting ranks early, which occasionally lets a lucky null
into the top four when the active signal is weak, yet reliably rescues
active coordinates from single-setting failures further down the list.
Aggregation is thus a stabilizer of inclusion rather than of exact
selection, which matches the intended first-stage use of a screen.

Overall, the quantile screen at a moderate level remains the strongest
detector when high quantiles and the limiting exponent share their
covariates and the signal is weak (M3); the aggregated tail screen is
competitive or better on M1 and M4 and is the only method that
recovers the tail-index-active set reliably in the presence of the M2
scale cluster, which distracts every fixed-quantile method at every
level. For a question about the limiting exponent itself, the two
rankings should be read side by side, as in
Section~\ref{sec:realdata}: variables high on one list and low on the
other are precisely where the two targets part ways.
