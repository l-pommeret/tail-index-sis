\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

Each coordinate is scored in $O(n\log n)$ time: one sort by empirical
rank, then a sliding window over the rank grid whose local Hill statistics
are maintained by a Fenwick tree indexed by global response ranks.
Covariates are generated and processed in blocks, so the total cost is
$O(pn\log n)$ time and $O(n)$ working memory beyond the scores. At
$(n,p)=(2000,1000)$ one complete screen takes under a second on a laptop
core. Scores are sorted increasingly, ties broken by coordinate index; a
coordinate whose grid is entirely underpopulated receives score $+\infty$.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

All four models share the covariate design and the active set
$\A=\{1,2,3,4\}$. Latent Gaussian AR(1) covariates
$Z_{ij}=\rho Z_{i,j-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\epsilon_{ij}$ are transformed to
$U_{ij}=\Phi(Z_{ij})$; the tail-index functions are evaluated at the
population transforms $U$, while the estimator sees only $Z$ and computes
its own empirical ranks. Responses are generated by quantile inversion,
\[
 Y=V^{-\gamma(U)}\,\ell(1/V,U),\qquad V\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1),
\]
the mechanism used by \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. We combine three
tail-index functions from increasing difficulty,
\begin{align*}
 \gamma_1(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_2(u)&=0.5\exp\bigl(-0.5\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j\bigr),\\
 \gamma_3(u)&=0.55\exp\bigl(-0.80\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4u_j
 -0.20\,u_1u_2-0.15\,u_3u_4\bigr),
\end{align*}
with two slowly varying functions in the forms of
\citet[Section~4.3]{gardesPodgorny2025},
\[
 \ell^{(1)}(s,u)=\bigl[1+\exp\{v(u)-s\}\bigr]^{-1},
 \qquad
 \ell^{(2)}(s,F)=e^{-1/(2s)}\,v_\kappa(F),
\]
where $v(u)=2.5\,(u_5+u_6+u_7+u_8)\in(0,10)$ matches the range of their
$B_1^\top x$. The scale factor of $\ell^{(2)}$ is carried by a single
latent standard normal $F$, independent of the active coordinates and not
itself observed, through
\[
 v_\kappa(F)=\exp\bigl\{\kappa\bigl(\Phi(F)-\tfrac12\bigr)\bigr\}
 \in[e^{-\kappa/2},e^{\kappa/2}],
\]
strictly positive and bounded, which keeps the model compatible with the
uniform constants in (C2). What the analyst observes are $s=20$ proxies of
$F$, occupying the coordinates $\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,\ldots,24\}$,
\[
 Z_{ij}=\lambda F_i+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\,\epsilon_{ij},
 \qquad j\in\A_{\rm scale},
 \qquad \lambda=0.7,
\]
so each is marginally standard normal like every other covariate and
correlates with the nuisance at $\lambda$. Two consequences make this
parameterization the useful one. First,
$\operatorname{Var}\{\log v_\kappa(F)\}=\kappa^{2}/12$ exactly, whatever
$s$ and $\lambda$: the magnitude of the nuisance is a property of $\kappa$
alone, so the number of scale covariates and the quality of each proxy can
be varied without changing how hard the tail index is to estimate.
Second, $\kappa^\star=0.4772$ is the value for which that standard
deviation, $\kappa^\star/\sqrt{12}=0.138$, equals the one produced at the
baseline design by the additive form
$\exp\{\kappa\sum_{j=5}^{8}(u_j-\tfrac12)\}$ of
\citet[Section~4.3]{gardesPodgorny2025} at their $\kappa=0.20$; the two
specifications therefore perturb the response by the same amount.
The four models are
\[
 \mathrm{M1}=(\gamma_1,\ell^{(1)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M2}=(\gamma_1,\ell^{(2)}_{\kappa^\star}),\quad
 \mathrm{M3}=(\gamma_2,\ell^{(1)}),\quad
 \mathrm{M4}=(\gamma_3,\ell^{(1)}).
\]
M1 is the reference. M2 keeps the tail index of M1 and changes only the
slowly varying factor: $\ell^{(2)}(s,F)\to v_\kappa(F)$ as $s\to\infty$,
so the tail index depends on $\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$ while the scale ---
hence every high finite quantile --- depends instead on the latent $F$,
and through it on the twenty observed proxies
$\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,\ldots,24\}$. Variables can therefore matter for a
$0.95$-quantile screen without entering the limiting exponent, by
construction, and here twenty of them do; Section~\ref{sec:comparison}
exploits this. M3 halves the exponent of the signal, weakening the
population gap from $0.186$ to $0.107$; M4 adds interactions among the
active coordinates while remaining detectable
(Proposition~\ref{prop:geometry}), with trimmed gap $0.173$.

The population gaps quoted above follow in closed form from the
construction. Conditioning on $U_j=t$ for an active $j$ and maximising
over the other three active coordinates places them at $0$, where the
interaction terms of $\gamma_3$ vanish; the upper envelope along the
fibre is therefore $c\,e^{-\beta t}$ for all three tail-index functions,
with $(c,\beta)$ equal to $(0.5,1)$, $(0.5,0.5)$ and $(0.55,0.80)$, while
an inactive coordinate has the flat envelope $c$. Averaging over the
trimmed grid $(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)$ gives
\[
 \Delta \;=\; c\Bigl[1-\frac{e^{-\beta\varepsilon}-e^{-\beta(1-\varepsilon)}}
 {\beta\,(1-2\varepsilon)}\Bigr],
\]
that is $0.186$, $0.107$ and $0.173$. The interaction coefficients of
$\gamma_3$ do not enter: they act in the interior of the tail-index
surface, not on its upper envelope, so M4 tests robustness to
non-additivity without being harder to detect on that account.

None of the four models belongs to the sufficient classes of
Appendix~\ref{app:examples}: their tail-index surfaces are continuous
with maximizers at a corner of the active cube, so the fibre-maximizer
laws have no atom and the projected quantiles acquire slow variation of
logarithmic type --- the boundary regime in which condition (E2) covers
only gaps large relative to $1/\log n$. At $n=2000$ the corresponding
bias modulus is of order one, larger than every population gap
($0.107$--$0.186$), so the finite-sample bounds of
Section~\ref{sec:estimation} are not numerically informative for these
designs. The simulations therefore probe the method beyond the regime
its theory certifies. Ranking nevertheless succeeds because much of the
local Hill bias is shared across coordinates --- every inactive
coordinate estimates the same projected mixture --- and the screen uses
only score differences; the experiments should be read as evidence
about this common-mode behaviour, which the theory does not exploit,
rather than as confirmation of the bounds.

Unless a factor is explicitly varied, the design is
\[
 n=2000,\qquad p=1000,\qquad \rho=0.25,\qquad d=20,
 \qquad \varepsilon=0.05,
\]
with 1000 Monte Carlo replications per cell in the comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:comparison}, and 200 in the tuning and sensitivity
studies that Section~\ref{sec:tuning} and Section~\ref{sec:factors}
carry over unchanged. Sure-$d$ denotes
$\Pp(R_{\max}\le d)$ with $R_{\max}=\max_{j\in\A}\mathrm{rank}(j)$;
binomial standard errors are at most $0.016$ on 1000 replications and
$0.036$ on 200.

\subsection{Tuning}\label{sec:tuning}

The two smoothing parameters are parameterized as
\[
 \alpha=n^{-a},\qquad h=n^{-b}/2,
\]
following the convention of \citet{gardesPodgorny2025}. We evaluate
Sure-20 for each of the four models over the crossed grid
$a\in\{.25,.30,.35,.40,.45,.50\}$,
$b\in\{0,.05,.10,.15,.20,.30,.40\}$ at the baseline design;
Figure~\ref{fig:tuning} shows the four heatmaps. Each replication draws one
dataset and scores it at all 42 tunings, so the cells of a heatmap are paired:
the comparisons that decide the selection carry no common variance, at the
price that the 42 cells are not independent and absolute differences across
distant cells should be read with that in mind.

Three features stand out. The row $a=0.25$ collapses for the three
$\ell^{(1)}$ models (Sure-20 at most $.110$, $.085$ and $.250$ for M1,
M3 and M4) but not for M2 (up to $.595$): at
$\alpha=n^{-0.25}\approx0.15$ the local Hill statistics reach into
quantile regions where the factor $\ell^{(1)}$, whose argument
$v(u)-s$ is dominated by the $U_5$--$U_8$ combination $v(u)\in(0,10)$
at moderate $s$, deforms the projected distributions; $\ell^{(2)}$, bounded
within $[e^{-0.24},e^{0.24}]$, produces no such deformation. Performance also degrades toward large $a$ and large $b$
together, where the local extreme count collapses, and the degenerate
bandwidth $b=0$, which retains half of the rank scale in every window,
is never optimal. In between, the row $a=0.30$ performs best for M1,
M3 and M4 simultaneously over $b\in\{.05,\ldots,.20\}$, with Sure-20
ranges $(.890$--$.965,\ .450$--$.585,\ .815$--$.925)$.
The M1 variation over this region is small ($0.075$, about two standard
errors of a difference); the M3 and M4 variations are larger ($0.135$ and
$0.110$), so the region is one of good rather than statistically
indistinguishable performance, and we select the value of $b$ at which
both M3 and M4 attain their grid maxima. We fix
\[
 (a^\star,b^\star)=(0.30,\ 0.15),
 \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
 \alpha=n^{-0.3},\quad h=n^{-0.15}/2,
\]
for every subsequent experiment, in simulation and in the application; at
$n=2000$ this gives $\alpha\approx0.098$, $h\approx0.160$, and about
$2n\alpha h\approx65$ effective local extremes per interior window,
whose width is $2h$. The same cell is also M1's grid maximum and the
maximum of the four-model average ($0.796$). No model is given its own
tuning.

Section~\ref{sec:comparison} also reports an aggregated form of the screen,
which ranks a coordinate by the smallest rank it attains over the nine
tunings $a\in\{.30,.35,.40\}\times b\in\{.10,.15,.20\}$. That block is
not centred on $(a^\star,b^\star)$ in $a$: the row $a=0.25$ is excluded
deliberately, since a rule that promotes a coordinate as soon as one tuning
ranks it well is degraded, not helped, by admitting tunings that fail.

M2 behaves like a damped M1: its Sure-20 reaches $0.710$ at
$(a^\star,b^\star)$ and its grid maximum, $0.760$, sits at the deeper
tail fraction $a=0.35$ with the same $b=0.15$ --- the direction
predicted by condition (E2), since a smaller $\alpha$ reduces the bias
contributed by $\ell^{(2)}$. M2 uses the common tuning, at a cost of
$0.05$ relative to its own best cell; its role in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} is to separate statistical targets.

Two caveats on this selection protocol apply throughout. Sure-20 is an
oracle criterion --- it requires knowing the active set --- so the
choice of $(a^\star,b^\star)$ is a simulation-calibrated default, not a
data-driven rule; no such rule is proposed in this paper, and the
half-sample stability diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:realdata} are the
practical substitute. Second, for the $\ell^{(1)}$ models $a^\star=0.30$ sits directly
above the collapsed row $a=0.25$, so moderate misspecification of $a$
toward larger tail fractions is the dangerous direction there ---
model-dependently, since M2 is robust at $a=0.25$; the real-data
stability grid includes $a=0.25$ deliberately.

\subsection{Influence of the design factors}\label{sec:factors}

This subsection uses Model M1 only, varies one factor at a time around the
baseline $(n,p,\rho,d)=(2000,1000,0.25,20)$, on a separate seed stream
from the tuning study. At the baseline, Sure-20 is $0.950$ and
the mean worst active rank is $9.2$; the same design cell appears in the
tuning grid ($0.935$) and in the comparison below ($0.925$), each from
its own replications, and the three values agree within Monte Carlo
error. Figure~\ref{fig:factors} displays
the three one-factor curves.

Sample size acts through the local extreme count, in the direction the rate suggests:
Sure-20 rises from $0.400$ at $n=1000$ to $0.950$ at $n=2000$ and
$1.000$ at $n=5000$. The ambient dimension degrades performance slowly:
$0.995$, $0.960$, $0.950$, $0.905$ at $p=100$, $500$, $1000$, $5000$
--- a five-fold increase of $p$ beyond the baseline costs less than five
points of screening probability, consistent with the logarithmic entry
of $p$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:sis}. Dependence helps in this design:
Sure-20 is $0.615$ at $\rho=0$ and reaches $1.000$ at $\rho\ge0.5$. The
active block $\{1,2,3,4\}$ is contiguous in the AR(1) chain, so positive
correlation concentrates observations near the diagonal of the active
cube, where the envelope is estimated; the direction of this effect is
design-specific and correlated inactive neighbours of the active block
can equally be promoted in finite samples.

Figure~\ref{fig:sured} shows $d\mapsto\Pp(R_{\max}\le d)$ at the
baseline; the ranking underlying the curve does not depend on $d$. The
curve starts at $0.805$ for $d=4$, which is exact recovery since
$|\A|=4$, passes $0.950$ at the default $d=20$, and reaches $0.990$ by
$d=100$. A moderate retained dimension therefore buys near-certain
inclusion, which is the intended reading of a screening guarantee.

\subsection{Comparison with competing screens}\label{sec:comparison}

Four rules are compared on the same 1000 simulated datasets per model at
the baseline design: the proposed tail-index screen with the tuning
$(a^\star,b^\star)$ fixed in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}; its aggregated form,
ranking by the smallest rank attained over the nine tunings of that section;
the
conditional-Pickands screen of \citet{yoshidaUmezu2026}, implemented from
their equations (2)--(4) with the Epanechnikov kernel and the tuning of
their numerical work transplanted proportionally
($h=1$, $k=\lfloor0.072\,n\rfloor$), on 25 equally spaced evaluation
points in $[.02,.98]$; the bandwidth sensitivity below varies $h$ only,
with $k$ kept at their ratio; and the quantile-adaptive SIS of
\citet{heWangHong2013}, using cubic B-spline marginal quantile regression
with three degrees of freedom, identical at every quantile level, its
utility being the empirical squared deviation of the fitted conditional
quantile from the marginal one. The two competitors rank large utilities
first; ours ranks small scores first. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} reports
the levels $\tau\in\{.90,.95,.99\}$, together with
$\mathbb{E}(R_{\max})$ and $\mathrm{Med}(R_{\max})$, whose gap measures the
asymmetry of the worst-active-rank distribution.

At $\tau=.95$, quantile SIS and the tail-index screen are close on M1 and
M4, the two models whose signal is strongest: Sure-20 $.997$ and $.994$
against $.985$ and $.980$ for the aggregated screen, which in turn leads on
exact recovery for M1 ($.882$ against $.830$). On M3, whose population gap is
the smallest of the four, quantile SIS is clearly ahead ($.873$ against
$.695$): the monotone $\gamma$ moves the entire upper distribution and the
quantile utility pools all $n=2000$ observations at a moderately extreme
level, while the tail-index statistic works from about $2n\alpha h\approx65$
local extremes per window. At $\tau=.99$ the ordering reverses on all four
models ($.674$, $.463$, $.103$, $.533$ against $.985$, $.866$, $.695$,
$.980$), and at $\tau=.90$ it reverses on M2. Aggregating over the nine
tunings improves the screen on every model, by $.031$ to $.183$ of Sure-20,
and shortens the right tail of $R_{\max}$ markedly --- on M2 the mean worst
rank falls from $54.4$ to $15.8$ while the median moves only from $8$ to $5$,
which is the signature of fewer catastrophic replications rather than a
uniform improvement.

The Pickands-based screen of \citet{yoshidaUmezu2026} trails on all four
models at the transplanted tuning, with mean worst ranks of $124$ to $320$.
Its scores are all well defined here, so the deficit is variance rather than
failed fits: it differences three conditional quantiles where the proposed
score averages $\lfloor\alpha m\rfloor$ log-spacings.

M2 separates the two statistical targets. Its tail index depends on
$\A_\gamma=\{1,2,3,4\}$ only, while the scale factor $v_{\kappa^\star}$
carries the latent $F$ --- and with it the twenty proxies
$\A_{\rm scale}=\{5,\ldots,24\}$ --- into every finite quantile.
Table~\ref{tab:composition} shows the composition of each method's four
leading positions. The quantile screen at $\tau=.90$ places on average
$3.70$ scale variables in its top four, and at $\tau=.95$ still $2.19$; the
aggregated tail-index screen places $0.17$. The consequence is visible in the
rank distribution rather than in a variance: at $\tau=.90$ the mean and
median worst active rank are $25.2$ and $24$, that is exactly behind the
twenty proxies, so the quantile screen recovers $\A_\gamma$ only once its
leading positions have been exhausted by variables that do not determine the
limiting exponent. This is correct behaviour for its own target --- those
variables genuinely move the $.90$ quantile --- and the point of the model is
that the two targets can be told apart: an analyst reading a $\tau=.90$
ranking as a statement about the tail would carry forward twenty variables
that have nothing to do with it. As $\tau$ increases the quantile target
approaches the tail index and the contamination falls ($0.27$ at
$\tau=.99$), but the variance cost rises faster: Sure-20 goes from $.593$ at
$\tau=.95$ to $.463$ at $\tau=.99$, against $.866$ for the aggregated screen.

\subsection{Sensitivity of quantile SIS to the level}\label{sec:tau}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} reports the quantile screen at
$\tau\in\{.90,.95,.99\}$ on common datasets, and two forms of sensitivity
combine there. First, changing $\tau$ changes the statistical target
$Q_\tau(Y\mid X)$: under M2, lower levels weight the scale variables more
heavily, which is visible in Table~\ref{tab:composition} --- $3.70$ of the
four leading positions at $\tau=.90$ against $0.27$ at $\tau=.99$. Second,
raising $\tau$ thins the information available for estimating the target:
every model peaks at $\tau=.95$ and collapses by $\tau=.99$
(Figure~\ref{fig:tau})
(M3: $.873$ to $.103$; M4: $.994$ to $.533$). The two act in opposite
directions on M2, where the contamination argues for a high level and the
variance for a moderate one, and no level resolves both: the best of the
three, $\tau=.95$, still reaches only $.593$ against $.866$ for the
aggregated tail-index screen. The
tail-index screen is subject to a bias--variance compromise of the same
kind through $\alpha$, but with a fixed asymptotic target: moving
$\alpha$ changes the threshold used to estimate $\gamma$, not the
definition of $\gamma$. Neither method is tuning-free; the tunings mean
different things.

The comparison supports a conditional recommendation. For a scientific
question about a fixed high quantile, or when high quantiles and the
limiting exponent can be expected to share their covariates, the
quantile screen at a moderate level exploits the full sample and is the
better tool. For a question about the limiting exponent itself ---
which covariates change the polynomial rate of decay --- the tail-index
screen targets that quantity directly, keeps scale variables out of its
leading positions, and avoids the variance collapse that affects the
quantile screen at genuinely extreme levels; aggregating it over a small
block of tunings costs nine passes of an $O(pn\log n)$ statistic and
removes much of the right tail of $R_{\max}$. Reading the two rankings
side by side, as in Section~\ref{sec:realdata}, identifies the
variables on which the two questions part ways.
